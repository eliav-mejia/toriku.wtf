Audiovisual copyright in Latin America: a quick map

Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Peru and the Andean Community: term, author remuneration and AI for co-producing and distributing shorts in the region.

November 15, 2026 · 2 min read · Toriku team

If your short is going to be co-produced, screened or licensed in other countries in the region, you need to know that each one has its own law. They share principles through international treaties, but they differ in term, in who counts as an author and in remuneration rights. This is an orientation map; for each contract, check the current law in WIPO's database.

What they share

  • They are all parties to the Berne Convention: your Mexican work is protected without registration in each country.
  • They all recognize moral and economic rights.
  • They all allow economic rights to be assigned by contract.

Term (general rule)

  • Mexico: author's life plus 100 years.
  • Colombia: author's life plus 80 years.
  • Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Peru: author's life plus 70 years.

This difference matters for film clubs and programmers: a classic film can be in the public domain in one country and protected in another.

Remuneration for directors and screenwriters

A strong trend in the region is to grant directors, screenwriters and actors a remuneration right for the public communication of their works, which they keep even if they assign the other rights to the producer. Colombia strengthened it with the so-called Pepe Sánchez Law (2017), Chile has laws recognizing rights for audiovisual performers and authors, and Argentina has collecting societies for directors. If your work is screened in those countries, it may generate income managed by local societies.

The Andean Community

Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru also apply Andean Decision 351, a common copyright regime. National laws complement it.

AI in the region

  • Brazil is debating AI bill PL 2338/2023, approved by the Senate and under review in the Chamber, which includes rules on using protected works to train models.
  • Mexico already has the Supreme Court's position on human authorship and the 2026 reform on voice and likeness.
  • Other countries are discussing bills or national strategies. The principle of human authorship recurs in almost every legal system.

Co-producing in the region

  • Define in the contract which law applies and which court resolves disputes.
  • Split territories: who exploits the work in each country.
  • Review co-production agreements and regional funds such as Ibermedia, which require certain participation percentages per country.
  • Clarify who collects collective management remuneration in each territory.

Distribution and piracy

Each country has its own mechanisms to remove unauthorized copies. In practice, global platforms apply their notice and takedown systems regardless of country. Register your work in Mexico and keep your chain of title: it is what you will be asked for anywhere.

Latin America is a common market by language, not by law. Read each territory's contract as if it were the first.

General information, not legal advice. Laws change and every case is different. Before signing, screening or publishing, consult an intellectual property lawyer in your country.
