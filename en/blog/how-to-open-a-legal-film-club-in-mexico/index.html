How to open a legal film club in Mexico: rights, licenses and permits

What the law says about public screenings, why «non-profit» is not enough, how to get licenses, ratings, music and venue permits.

November 15, 2026 · 3 min read · Toriku team

Film clubs have a long tradition in Mexico: universities, cultural centers, cafés and neighborhood collectives programming films that never reach commercial theaters. Opening one is one of the best ways to build an audience. Doing it legally is simpler than it looks, but you have to understand one central idea.

The central idea: screening is communicating to the public

When you screen a film to an audience beyond your family circle, you are making a public communication of the work. Mexico's Federal Copyright Law reserves that right for the rights holder: you need their authorization.

This applies even if:

  • Admission is free.
  • The film club is non-profit.
  • You own the DVD or a subscription to the platform.

Buying a copy or paying for a streaming service gives you the right to watch it privately, not to screen it. Platform terms say so explicitly.

How to get authorization

  1. Ask the director or the production company. For shorts and independent Mexican films, it is the most direct route. Many filmmakers authorize film club screenings for free or for a symbolic fee in exchange for credit and promotion. Ask in writing, even if it is just an email.
  2. Contact the distributor in Mexico. For commercial films, the local distributor usually handles non-theatrical screening licenses. Ask about per-screening fees.
  3. Public performance licensing agencies. In several countries there are companies representing studio catalogs for non-commercial screenings. Ask whether they operate in Mexico and which titles they cover.
  4. Institutional programs. Some cultural institutions have catalogs for alternative circuits. Ask your state's culture ministry.
  5. Public domain and open licenses. See our note on programming: in Mexico the public domain arrives late, but there are options.

Institutions such as UNAM require their film clubs to program legitimate material and keep proof of the license or authorization. It is good practice for any film club.

Ratings

Films screened publicly in Mexico have an age rating. Respect it in your programming and promotion: if you screen a film rated for adults, do not advertise it for all audiences.

Music

The music inside a film is usually covered by the film's license. But if your film club plays background music before or after the screening, that is another public communication that may require payment to collecting societies. Ask.

The venue

  • Local permits: if you operate in a venue, check your borough's or municipality's requirements for events and the civil protection capacity limit.
  • If you charge admission or sell food, check your tax situation and business permits.
  • Safety: evacuation routes, a fire extinguisher and one person in charge per screening.

The new Film Law

Mexico's 2026 Federal Law of Film and Audiovisual Media mentions screening in alternative spaces as well as cinemas. When its regulations are published, check whether they set rules or support for film clubs and community circuits.

Starter checklist

  1. Define your audience and your programming line.
  2. Choose the venue and check permits and capacity.
  3. Draw up a three-month calendar.
  4. Get written authorization for each title.
  5. Prepare a sheet per screening: title, year, director, rating, license.
  6. Host a conversation after each screening.

A legal film club is an ally of filmmakers, not a competitor. Paying or asking permission is how films keep existing.

General information, not legal advice. Laws change and every case is different. Before signing, screening or publishing, consult an intellectual property lawyer in your country.
