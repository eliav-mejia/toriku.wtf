Film copyright in Mexico: who owns your short?

Who the authors of an audiovisual work are under Mexico's copyright law, what the producer acquires, moral and economic rights, INDAUTOR registration and basic contracts.

November 15, 2026 · 3 min read · Toriku team

You finished your short. Is it yours? It depends on who wrote it, who directed it, who composed the music and, above all, on what they signed. Mexico's Federal Copyright Law (LFDA) has specific rules for audiovisual works that are worth knowing before you shoot.

Who the authors are

The LFDA recognizes as authors of an audiovisual work:

  • The director.
  • The authors of the story, adaptation, script or dialogue.
  • The authors of the musical compositions created for the work.
  • The cinematographer (director of photography).
  • The authors of animated drawings or cartoons, in animated works.

In other words: a film has several authors at once. That is why contracts are so important.

The producer's role

The producer is the one who takes the initiative, coordinates and is responsible for making the work. Unless otherwise agreed, the law presumes that the authors assign to the producer, exclusively, the economic rights to reproduce, distribute, publicly communicate and subtitle the work. That presumption helps, but it does not replace a written contract.

Moral and economic rights

  • Moral rights: the right to be recognized as the author and to object to distortions of the work. They are perpetual, cannot be waived and cannot be transferred. Nobody can buy them from you.
  • Economic rights: the right to exploit the work (copy, distribute, screen, broadcast it). They can be assigned or licensed, in writing, for a set term and a fee.

In Mexico, economic rights generally last the author's life plus one hundred years.

Do you need to register?

The work is protected from the moment it is fixed in a medium, without registration. Even so, registering with INDAUTOR is highly recommended: it creates a presumption of authorship and date that helps with any dispute, negotiation with platforms or application to public funds. Register the script before you show it and the finished work at the end.

Commissioned works

If a brand or client commissions a video from you, the law has special rules on who holds certain rights. In practice, everything is defined in the contract: who owns it, for which media, in which territories and for how long. If it is not in writing, there will be an argument.

Music, actors and locations

  • Music: you need a synchronization license (the composition) and a master license (the recording). A commercial song without a license can block your short at festivals and on platforms.
  • Actors: a contract assigning image and performance rights and, since 2026, a specific AI clause: the LFDA reform requires written authorization to clone or reproduce their voice or image with AI.
  • Locations: written permission from the owner, especially if the place is recognizable.
  • Works that appear in frame: protected murals, sculptures, logos or photographs may require permission if they are the focus of the shot.

The chain of title

When you sell or license your short, you will be asked to prove you hold all the rights. That collection of contracts is called the chain of title. Keep it in a folder from day one:

  1. Script contract or story assignment.
  2. Creative crew contracts assigning rights to the producer.
  3. Actor contracts and releases.
  4. Music licenses.
  5. Location permits.
  6. INDAUTOR registration.

In a short made among friends, a contract feels like overkill. Until the short wins a festival and a platform wants to buy it. Sign before you shoot.

General information, not legal advice. Laws change and every case is different. Before signing, screening or publishing, consult an intellectual property lawyer in your country.
