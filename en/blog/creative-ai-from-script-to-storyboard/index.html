Creative AI: from script to storyboard without losing your voice

How to use artificial intelligence in a short film's pre-production as an assistant, not an author: ideas, script, storyboard, previsualization and animatic.

November 15, 2026 · 3 min read · Toriku team

The question is no longer whether you will use artificial intelligence in your next short film, but where. Almost every editing, color and sound tool now ships with AI features, and image and video generators are one click away. The difference between an AI-assisted short that works and one that feels generic is almost never the tool: it is who makes the decisions.

At Toriku we use a simple rule: AI proposes, the person decides. That rule is not just about aesthetics. In Mexico, the Supreme Court ruled in 2025 that a work generated exclusively by AI has no author and cannot be registered with INDAUTOR, the national copyright office. What is protected is what you contribute: your selection, your arrangement, your writing, your images.

1. Ideas: use AI to open up, not to close down

At the idea stage, a language model works as a brainstorming partner that never gets tired. It is good for:

  • Generating twenty variations of a premise so you can throw out nineteen.
  • Asking it «what cliché is hiding in this idea?» and writing the exact opposite.
  • Researching the context of an era, a trade or a neighborhood before you go and talk to real people.

What does not work: asking it «write me a short film about Mexico City» and shooting the result as is. You will get an average of everything the model has read, and averages do not win festivals.

2. Script: the first draft is yours

Write the first draft yourself, even if it is bad. Then you can ask the AI to read it like a script reader: where the pace drops, which lines over-explain, which scene is unnecessary. Treat its notes like those of a workshop peer: some help, many do not.

Save dated versions. If someone questions your authorship tomorrow, your draft history is the best proof that the work is yours.

3. Storyboard and previsualization

This is where AI saves days. With an image generator you can:

  • Test framing and palettes before renting gear.
  • Build an animatic from still frames and a temp sound track to measure the real length of each scene.
  • Show a client or a funding body how a location's light will look at a given time of day.

Two cautions: do not copy the recognizable style of a living artist in your pitch material, and do not upload photos of real people without their permission. For the crew, a storyboard is a communication tool; nobody expects it to be final art.

4. The shoot: AI to plan, people to film

On set, AI is more useful for logistics than for the camera: script breakdown, shooting schedule, props lists, daylight timings. Decisions about performance, framing and rhythm still belong to the crew. A short feels alive when there is human risk in front of the camera.

5. Post-production: where AI is already standard

  • Audio cleanup: noise reduction and voice separation.
  • Transcription and subtitles: then check names and slang by hand.
  • Rotoscoping and reframing: automatic masks that used to take hours.
  • Conforming and searching takes by content.

All of this is AI as a tool, just as autofocus once was. It does not change the authorship of the work.

6. What you should document

Keep a short log: which tool you used, in which scene and what for. It will help with festival forms that already ask about AI use, with the credits, and with any platform that asks you to disclose synthetic content.

Toriku rule: if you remove the AI from your process and the short is still yours, you are on the right track. If nothing is left once you remove it, the short is not yours yet.

To start tomorrow

  1. Write the premise in one line, without AI.
  2. Ask for twenty variations and keep the one that makes you uncomfortable.
  3. Write the draft yourself.
  4. Use AI for the animatic and the shooting schedule.
  5. Shoot with people and document what you used.
