When is it ethical to use AI in a short film? A practical guide

Clear criteria to decide when artificial intelligence adds value and when it crosses a line: consent, voices, faces, training data and transparency.

November 15, 2026 · 3 min read · Toriku team

AI ethics in film is not settled with a «yes» or a «no». It is settled with concrete questions before each use. This is the checklist we use at Toriku to decide.

The five questions

  1. Is a real person's face, body or voice being used? If so, you need their written consent, specific to that use.
  2. Am I replacing work someone would do for fair pay? It is not always unethical, but it has to be said and weighed.
  3. Could the audience mistake the synthetic for the real? If so, label it.
  4. Am I imitating the recognizable style of a living artist? If so, find another way or ask for permission.
  5. Do I know where the tool's training data came from? If not, avoid it for final commercial pieces.

When yes

  • Craft tools: audio cleanup, subtitles, rotoscoping, stabilization, searching takes. They are the modern equivalent of the editing bench.
  • Previsualization: storyboards, animatics and color tests that nobody will see as the final work.
  • Effects that used to be impossible on your budget, when they serve the story and the credit is clear.
  • Accessibility: audio description and subtitles so more people can see your work, always with human review.

When no

  • Cloning someone's voice or image without permission. In Mexico this is no longer only an ethical matter: the reform to the Federal Copyright Law published in May 2026 requires prior written authorization to reproduce a person's voice or image identifiably with AI.
  • Replacing human dubbing of foreign works. Mexico's new Federal Law of Film and Audiovisual Media states that dubbing into national languages is done by people.
  • Deepfakes that can pass as real, especially of public figures, without a clear label.
  • Presenting something generated as «handmade». Lying about the process destroys the trust of audiences and festivals.

The gray zone: digital actors and extras

Generating crowds or background extras with AI is increasingly common. A reasonable criterion: if the character is not identifiable and does not replace a speaking role, the ethical risk is lower. If a character has a face, a name and dialogue, think about who would have played them and why you are choosing not to hire them.

The 2023 Hollywood union agreements set out an idea worth adopting in Mexico even if no one forces you to: informed consent and payment every time a digital replica of an actor is created or used.

Transparency: how to say it without ruining the magic

You do not need a sign on every shot. It is enough to:

  • Add a line in the credits: «Background images in scenes 4 and 7 generated with AI and retouched by the crew».
  • Tick the altered or synthetic content box when the platform asks for it.
  • Answer festival forms truthfully.

If you distribute in Europe, from August 2, 2026 the EU AI Act requires anyone publishing a deepfake to state that the content was artificially generated or manipulated, with nuances for obviously artistic and fictional works. Spain is also passing its own law on the good use of AI with mandatory labeling.

Contracts: put AI in writing

Many problems come from contracts written before generative AI. In every contract with actors, voice talent, photographers and screenwriters, add:

  • Whether creating digital replicas is allowed, for what and for how long.
  • Whether footage can be used to train models.
  • How each additional use is paid.

The question that sums it all up: if the person affected saw exactly how you used AI, would they think it fair? If in doubt, ask them.

General information, not legal advice. Laws change and every case is different. Before signing, screening or publishing, consult an intellectual property lawyer in your country.
