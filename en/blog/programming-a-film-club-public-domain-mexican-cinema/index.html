Programming a film club: public domain, open licenses and Mexican cinema

Which films you can screen, why the public domain arrives late in Mexico, how to use Creative Commons licenses and how to build series with Mexican shorts.

November 15, 2026 · 2 min read · Toriku team

Once you know you need authorization to screen, the question is what to program. A good film club mixes well-known titles with discoveries, and always has a reason for each screening.

The public domain arrives late in Mexico

In Mexico, economic rights generally last the author's life plus one hundred years. On top of that, a film has several authors (director, screenwriter, composer, cinematographer). In practice, that means almost all Golden Age Mexican cinema is still protected in Mexico.

Beware of a common mistake: many films appear as «public domain» on US websites because protection works differently there. The public domain is territorial: what is free in one country may be protected in another. If you screen in Mexico, Mexican law applies.

Open licenses

Some filmmakers publish their work under Creative Commons licenses. Read carefully which one:

  • CC BY: you can screen it crediting the author.
  • CC BY-NC: you can screen it for non-commercial purposes. If you charge admission, check whether you comply.
  • CC BY-ND: you cannot modify the work (no cuts, no dubbing).
  • CC BY-SA: if you make a derivative work, it must be shared alike.

Save a screenshot of the license on the day you program the screening.

The best source: Mexican short films

Shorts are ideal for a film club:

  • Several fit in one screening, and they let you build themed series.
  • Their directors are usually happy for them to be seen and often to join the conversation.
  • You ask for authorization directly, by email.

Look in the catalogs of festivals, film schools and collectives. Ask for a good-quality file and the subtitles.

Series ideas

  • Mexico City in shorts: neighborhoods, trades, transport.
  • Festival winners: shorts awarded in the last five years, with their filmmakers' permission.
  • Documentaries by Mexican women filmmakers.
  • Film and video games: shorts made with game engines and a conversation about GTA VI as a cultural phenomenon.
  • AI and authorship: shorts that openly use AI, followed by a debate on ethics.
  • First shorts: the first works of directors who are now well known, if their production companies authorize it.

How to present a screening

  1. A brief introduction: why this film, today.
  2. The screening, without interruptions.
  3. A guided conversation with two or three open questions.
  4. If the filmmaker comes, let them speak at the end, not at the start.

Communication

  • Announce the title, year, director, running time and rating.
  • Credit whoever authorized the screening.
  • Ask attendees to sign up if they want to receive the program: your mailing list is your audience.

Projection formats

For a film club, a good-quality file (ProRes or high-bitrate H.264), a projector bright enough for your room and decent sound are enough. Always test the full copy before the screening.

Programming is curating: every title should answer «why watch this together?». If the answer is good, the audience comes back.

General information, not legal advice. Laws change and every case is different. Before signing, screening or publishing, consult an intellectual property lawyer in your country.
