How to make a video essay without infringing copyright (and get it watched)

Quotation in Mexico, the quotation right in Spain and fair use in the US: how much third-party footage to use in a video essay, how to structure it and how to respond to Content ID.

November 15, 2026 · 3 min read · Toriku team

The video essay is one of the most-watched formats about film. It is also one of the formats that gets the most copyright claims. The good news: almost every law protects criticism. The bad news: you have to do it right.

What each law allows

  • Mexico: the LFDA allows, without authorization or payment, the reproduction of parts of a work for criticism and research, as long as it does not affect the normal exploitation of the work and the source and author are cited.
  • Spain: the LPI allows including fragments of other people's works for analysis, commentary or critical judgment, citing the source and author, to the extent justified by that purpose.
  • United States: fair use protects criticism and commentary, weighing the purpose, the amount used and the effect on the market for the work.

The common denominator: the excerpt must serve your analysis, not the other way around.

Practical rules

  1. Short excerpts. Use only what you need to illustrate the point. No percentage is legally safe, but a few-second clip you comment on is very different from a full scene.
  2. Your voice on top. Talk about the excerpt, point things out, compare. A clip without commentary is hard to defend.
  3. Do not replace the film. If someone can watch your video instead of the film, you crossed the line. Avoid recapping the whole ending with footage.
  4. Always credit: title, director, year and distributor on screen or in the description.
  5. Avoid the film's music as a background. It is the most common cause of automatic claims. Use licensed or original music.
  6. Your own thumbnail. A frame from the film as a thumbnail is riskier than your own design with one recognizable element.

A structure that works

  • Hook (0–15 s): your thesis in one sentence.
  • Context (1 min): who made it, where it comes from, what the audience expected.
  • Analysis (the bulk): three ideas, each with one or two short excerpts.
  • Verdict: your opinion, clearly.
  • Close: who you recommend it to and why.

Official trailers

Trailers are promotional material distributors want to circulate, and they are often used in release coverage. Even so, they are still protected works: apply the same excerpt and commentary rules. The safest option is to link or embed the official trailer from the distributor's channel rather than re-uploading it.

If you get a Content ID claim

  • A claim is not a penalty: it may limit the video's monetization or availability.
  • If you believe your use is legitimate, you can dispute it explaining why: criticism, short excerpt, commentary, quotation.
  • If the dispute is rejected and you escalate to an appeal, the rights holder can request a formal takedown, which can result in a strike against your channel. Decide calmly.
  • Trim or mute the claimed part if it is irrelevant to your analysis: YouTube offers tools to do this without re-uploading.

Reviews of new releases

If you review films in theaters, respect embargoes: if you were invited to a press screening, do not publish before the agreed date. And if you have not seen the film, do not review it: make an expectations piece and say so clearly.

A good video essay uses the film to think, not to reproduce it. That is the best legal defense and also the best reason for someone to watch you.

General information, not legal advice. Laws change and every case is different. Before signing, screening or publishing, consult an intellectual property lawyer in your country.
