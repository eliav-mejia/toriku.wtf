Industry standards: DCP, color, audio and subtitles

What festivals, cinemas and platforms ask for when you deliver a short: DCP, color space, loudness, subtitles and paperwork. A checklist so delivery does not fail.

November 15, 2026 · 3 min read · Toriku team

A short can be brilliant and still be left out of a festival because of a badly delivered file. Technical standards are not bureaucracy: they make sure your film looks and sounds the same in a cinema in Morelia, on a TV in Madrid or on a phone in Monterrey. This is the minimum checklist.

Projection formats: the DCP

Digital cinemas project a DCP (Digital Cinema Package), defined by the DCI specification. What you need to know:

  • Picture in JPEG 2000 inside MXF files, in the XYZ color space.
  • 2K (2048×1080) or 4K (4096×2160) container resolutions. A short in flat 1.85 uses 1998×1080 in 2K; one in scope 2.39 uses 2048×858.
  • PCM audio at 48 kHz, 24 bits, usually 5.1 or stereo.
  • Today the SMPTE DCP standard is preferred over the old Interop.

Many festivals also accept a ProRes file for projection or for selection. Read the call for entries: each one says so.

Color: choose your space before grading

  • Rec.709 with gamma 2.4 is still the reference for television and most SDR deliveries.
  • DCI-P3 is the cinema color space.
  • HDR (PQ, Dolby Vision, HLG) is requested by some platforms for large productions.
  • ACES is a workflow that makes it easier to go from one camera to several destinations without losing information. For a short with several cameras, it is worth it.

Grade on a calibrated monitor and deliver one version for each destination. A single version «for everything» almost always looks wrong somewhere.

Audio: loudness, not volume

The most common mistake in independent shorts is delivering a mix that is too loud or too quiet. Standards measure integrated loudness:

  • European television: EBU R 128, −23 LUFS.
  • US television: ATSC A/85, −24 LKFS.
  • Platforms such as Netflix publish their own loudness target measured on dialogue.
  • For the cinema you mix at a reference monitoring level, not to a LUFS target.

Always ask the mixer for the separate tracks (dialogue, music, effects) as well as the final mix. They are called stems, and you will be asked for them for dubbing and international versions.

Subtitles

  • SRT for the web and most festivals.
  • TTML / IMSC for large platforms.
  • Two lines maximum, around 42 characters per line, and enough reading time.
  • Make an English version even if your short is in Spanish: it opens twice as many festivals.

Documents they always ask for

  • Licensed music list (music cue sheet).
  • Dialogue list.
  • Full credits and technical specs.
  • Chain of title: script, music, cast and location contracts.
  • High-resolution stills and poster.
  • Short (one-line) and long (one-paragraph) synopsis, in Spanish and English.

Cameras and source material

Large platforms publish lists of approved cameras and ask for capture at a minimum resolution. For festivals and social media there is no such restriction, but it pays to record with the highest-quality codec your budget allows: color grading and stabilization will thank you.

Before you send anything: watch your whole short, start to finish, in the exact file you are going to deliver. It is the only way to catch the black frame or the out-of-sync subtitle nobody noticed.
