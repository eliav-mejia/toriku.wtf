Filming in Mexico City: permits, locations and safety

When you need notice or a permit to film on Mexico City's public streets, monuments, transport, drones, insurance and how to work with neighbors.

November 15, 2026 · 2 min read · Toriku team

Mexico City is one of the world's great film sets: architecture from every era, neighborhoods with a strong identity and an experienced audiovisual industry. Filming here is possible and regulated. This guide sums up what to check before you take the camera onto the street.

The Film Commission

The Mexico City Film Commission coordinates the paperwork for filming on public streets and in city spaces. Depending on the size of your production, what you need may be a notice or a permit. In general:

  • A small shoot that does not close streets or take up space with gear usually requires less paperwork.
  • If you close streets, park production vehicles, or use cranes or generators, you need a permit and coordination with the authorities.

Requirements and fees change, so always check the Commission's website before planning.

Special spaces

  • Monuments and archaeological sites: require a permit from INAH, the national institute of anthropology and history, which may charge fees for commercial filming.
  • Artistic heritage buildings such as theaters and museums: check with the institution that runs them.
  • The Metro and public transport: have their own authorization procedures.
  • Parks and plazas: check with the borough or the authority in charge of the space.

Private property

To film in homes, shops or offices you need a written contract or permission from the owner, with dates, hours, permitted areas and liability for damage. If the facade or interior is recognizable, include authorization to show it.

Drones

Flying a drone for filming is regulated by the civil aviation authority (AFAC). Check the equipment registration, restricted zones (near the airport and strategic facilities the restriction is strict) and the rules for flying over people. In dense urban areas, hire an experienced operator.

Insurance

Production insurance covers damage to equipment, to third parties and accidents involving the crew. Many locations and gear rental companies require it. For small shorts there are per-shoot-day policies.

Neighbors and community

A disruptive shoot can end with a police patrol even if you have a permit. Good practice:

  • Notify neighbors in advance, in writing, with dates and a contact number.
  • Do not block garages or entrances.
  • Respect noise hours.
  • Leave the place cleaner than you found it.
  • If you film in a neighborhood, hire and credit local people when you can.

Safety

  • Plan the routes in and out.
  • Keep the gear insured and never leave it unattended.
  • Have one person responsible for safety each day.
  • Share the location with the crew and set a meeting point.

Image rights on the street

People who appear incidentally in the background usually do not require authorization, but if someone is identifiable and the focus of a shot, get their signature. See our note on film copyright in Mexico.
